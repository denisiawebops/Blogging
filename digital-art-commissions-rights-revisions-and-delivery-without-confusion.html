Digital Art Commissions: Rights, Revisions and Delivery Without Confusion

A practical guide for digital artists to structure commissions, manage intellectual property rights, and streamline delivery to avoid client disputes.

The excitement of landing a first big commission often vanishes the moment a client asks for 'one more quick change' for the fifth time, or discovers the artwork on a commercial product they weren't authorized to create. For many digital artists in India and globally, the gap between creative skill and business operation leads to 'scope creep'—where the project expands without a corresponding increase in pay. The pain isn't the drawing; it's the ambiguity of the agreement. To earn money with digital art sustainably, you must move from a 'per-piece' mindset to a professional service framework. This means defining exactly who owns the pixels, how many times a client can ask for changes, and what constitutes a final delivery. By establishing these boundaries upfront, you protect your time and your intellectual property while providing a predictable experience for your clients.

Quick answer

To earn money with digital art, artists typically offer custom commissions, sell licenses for commercial use, or create a hybrid of bespoke work and pre-made assets. Success depends on a structured workflow: clearly defining the scope of work, setting a limit on revisions, distinguishing between personal and commercial usage rights, and using a milestone-based payment system to ensure financial security throughout the creative process.

  • Separate personal use rights from commercial ownership to scale pricing.
  • Limit revisions to a specific number (e.g., two or three) to prevent infinite scope creep.
  • Use milestone payments (deposit and final) to reduce financial risk.
  • Define delivery formats (PNG, PSD, TIFF) explicitly to avoid technical back-and-forth.

Where the work gets stuck

  • Endless revision loops that erode the hourly profit of a project.
  • Clients using personal-commission art for commercial profit without additional payment.
  • Vague delivery expectations leading to requests for source files not included in the price.

Defining Usage Rights: Personal vs. Commercial

One of the most common points of failure in digital art commissions is the assumption that 'paying for the art' equals 'owning the copyright.' In professional practice, there is a sharp distinction between a personal commission and a commercial license.

Personal use generally means the client can use the art for their own enjoyment, such as a social media avatar or a private print. They cannot use it to promote a business, sell merchandise, or include it in a paid product. Commercial use allows the client to generate revenue from the artwork, which typically commands a higher price point because the art is now a business asset.

Artists should clearly state that the copyright remains with the creator unless a full transfer of ownership is explicitly purchased. This allows the artist to keep the work in their portfolio and potentially license it again in different contexts if the contract allows.

  • Personal Use: Social media profiles, personal wallpapers, private gifts.
  • Commercial Use: Book covers, game assets, merchandise, brand logos.
  • Exclusive Rights: Total transfer of ownership, usually the most expensive option.
  • Limited License: Use allowed for a specific time frame or a specific region.

Managing the Revision Cycle

Revisions are necessary, but unlimited revisions are a profit killer. Without a defined limit, a project can stretch from ten hours to forty without any additional compensation. The key is to tie revisions to specific project milestones.

The most effective approach is to offer revisions at the 'sketch' or 'blocking' phase. Changes to composition, anatomy, or posing are easy to fix early on. Once the artist has moved to final rendering and coloring, significant changes should be treated as a 're-draw' and billed as an additional fee.

Clear communication during the feedback loop is essential. Asking the client to provide a bulleted list of changes rather than vague feedback like 'make it feel more energetic' prevents wasted hours of guesswork.

  • Milestone 1: Rough Sketch (Maximum 2 revisions).
  • Milestone 2: Flat Colors/Lighting (Maximum 1 revision).
  • Final Polish: Minor tweaks only.
  • Out-of-Scope: Major structural changes after Milestone 1 approval incur extra costs.

Technical Delivery and File Specifications

A commission is not complete until the client has the files they need in the format they can use. Delivering a low-resolution JPEG when the client needs a high-resolution print file leads to frustration and perceived unprofessionalism.

Professional delivery involves providing a package of files. This typically includes a high-resolution version for printing, a web-optimized version for social media, and potentially a version with a transparent background (PNG).

The question of 'source files' (such as .PSD or .Procreate files) is a critical business decision. Many artists charge extra for source files because they reveal the artist's technical process and allow the client to modify the work without the artist's involvement.

  • Raster formats: PNG for transparency, JPEG for web, TIFF for high-end print.
  • Resolution: Minimum 300 DPI for print work.
  • Color Space: RGB for screens, CMYK for physical printing.
  • Source Files: Layered files provided only upon specific agreement or additional fee.

Structuring the Financial Workflow

To avoid the risk of 'ghosting'—where a client disappears after the work is finished—artists must move away from 100% payment upon completion. A milestone-based payment structure aligns the client's investment with the artist's effort.

A common professional standard is the 50/50 split: 50% upfront as a non-refundable deposit to secure the slot and cover initial sketching time, and 50% upon approval of the final watermarked preview.

Using watermarks is a vital safety measure. Final high-resolution, non-watermarked files should only be delivered after the final payment has been confirmed in the artist's account.

  • Deposit: Secures the calendar slot and covers initial research/sketching.
  • Approval: Final payment triggered by a low-res watermarked preview.
  • Release: High-res files sent only after payment clearance.
  • Late Fees: Optional terms for corporate clients to ensure timely payment.

From friction to a smoother workflow

  1. Ambiguous requests leading to wasted sketching time.

    Implement a mandatory commission brief covering character details, color palette, and intended use.

    Measure: Reduction in time spent on the initial rough sketch phase.

  2. Payment delays or cancellations after work has started.

    Require a non-refundable deposit (e.g., 50% upfront) before beginning any conceptual work.

    Measure: Percentage of projects started with secured funds.

  3. Confusion over final file types and ownership rights.

    Provide a delivery checklist specifying file formats and a written usage license (Personal vs Commercial).

    Measure: Decrease in post-delivery support queries regarding file types.

An illustrative example

Hypothetical Case: 'The Game Asset Project'. An artist is hired to create three character portraits. The agreement specifies: 1. Scope: Three 2000px PNGs. 2. Rights: Commercial license for use in one indie game (no resale of art). 3. Revisions: Two rounds of changes at the sketch stage; one final color tweak. 4. Delivery: Final files released upon payment of the remaining 50% balance. By labeling these terms, the artist avoids the 'just one more change' trap and ensures the client cannot sell the portraits as standalone stickers without a new contract.

Frequently asked questions

Should I provide my source files (.PSD, .CLIP) to the client?

This depends on your business model. Many artists treat source files as a premium add-on because they provide the client with the ability to edit the work and reveal your professional techniques. If you include them in the base price, ensure the license clearly states that the client cannot resell those layers as assets.

How do I handle a client who wants 'unlimited' revisions?

Avoid offering unlimited revisions. Instead, define a set number of revision rounds (e.g., two rounds of changes) included in the price. Clearly state that any changes requested after these rounds, or changes that alter the original agreed-upon brief, will be billed as an hourly add-on or a flat 'revision fee'.
